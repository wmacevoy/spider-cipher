The Spider Cipher

Ten cards, a little modular arithmetic, and a very large graph

Opening: hold up a deck of cards. Tonight this deck is a computer, a key, and a point in a graph with about 1048 vertices. We start by doing the cipher by hand with ten cards, then look at it as math: permutations, a Cayley graph, and three facts about that graph. The picture behind the title is the real graph for a 4-card deck; we’ll see it again.

Why encrypt with a deck of cards?

History

Solitaire, 1999

Bruce Schneier’s card cipher for Neal Stephenson’s Cryptonomicon. Elegant, but statistically leaky.

Trust

No hidden hardware

You can’t audit a chip. You can audit forty cards on a table.

Teaching

You can hold it

Key, keystream, and state are all physical: you can see every step.

Spider is a hand cipher built to be practical to run, and simple enough to analyze as mathematics.

Solitaire (Schneier, written for Cryptonomicon) showed you can build a stream cipher from a deck of cards, but it was found to be biased (Crowley 1999; Shiu 2019). Spider is named after the solitaire game Spider. Design goals: hand-operable, a key big enough to matter (a 40-card deck holds about 159 bits), and rules simple enough that we can prove things about them. Keep this short, about 2 minutes.

Hand out the 10-card decks now if you have them. The real cipher uses 40 cards (0–39, suit as tens digit, rank as ones digit) and adds padding and a letter encoding. We skip all of that: the core idea is the same. Have everyone put their deck in the order 3 7 0 9 4 1 8 5 2 6, top card first.

Two moves

Cut

Move cards from the top to the bottom, as one block, until a chosen card is on top.

Back-front shuffle

Deal into a new pile: the first card starts it, then alternate on top, underneath, on top…

Click a card to cut on it; Enter does “cut on 3” and then shuffles. Then demonstrate with real cards. The cut is a rotation: (tcd)i = di+c. The back-front (Monge) shuffle sends card 2i to position n/2 + i and card 2i+1 to position n/2 − 1 − i. From sorted: 0123456789 → 9753102468, odds rise in reverse, evens sink in order. Fun fact: the shuffle alone has order 6 on ten cards and 27 on forty. The shuffle never looks at the cards; only the cut depends on anything.

This is the whole cipher (minus padding and letter encoding). Step 2 is the clever part: the cut depends on the plaintext x, so the message itself steers the deck. Code: spider_cipher_scramble in core/src/spider_cipher.c. Ask: why does the receiver use x, not y, for the cut?

Why the receiver can follow

Same pad

Same deck, so the receiver reads the same pad and gets x = y − pad.

Same move

Now they know x, so they make the same cut and shuffle. The decks stay in lockstep.

No repeats

With the same key, 5555 encrypts to 9024.

The deck is the state.
The message picks the next state.

Have pairs swap: one encrypts a 3-digit message from the key deck, the other decrypts. The message feeds back into the state (like an autokey cipher), which is why the same digit doesn’t give the same output. Bridge to the math: if the deck is the state, the set of all states is the set of all orderings, and each step is a move from one ordering to another.

A deck is a permutation

10 cards

10! = 3,628,800

40 cards

40! ≈ 8.2 × 1047 ≈ 2159

A 40-card deck holds a 159-bit key, and you can shuffle it by hand.

Each deck order is an element of the symmetric group Sn: the deck d = [d0, …, dn−1] lists the card in each position. 40! = 8.16 × 1047, and log2(40!) ≈ 159.16. The sorted deck 0, 1, …, n−1 is the identity e. From now on: n cards, n even (the 10-card toy and the 40-card cipher both qualify).

Each step is one of n fixed moves

Cut by k

tk

Rotate: (tkd)i = di+k. A cyclic group of order n.

Shuffle

β

One fixed permutation: the back-front deal.

One step

gk = β tk

Cut, then shuffle. k = 0, 1, …, n − 1.

The key and the message decide which k, but the menu never changes: exactly n permutations.

In the cipher, k is the position of the card (top + x). For the math, forget why k was chosen: from any deck there are exactly n possible next decks, g0d, …, gn−1d. Note g0 = β. Notation follows foundations.ltx (gc = β∘tc); generators act on the left, on positions.

The spider graph

Vertices

All n! decks.

Arrows

d → gkd for each cut k: n out and n in at every deck.

Name

The Cayley graph Cay(Sn, {gk}).

Encrypting a message is a walk on this graph.
The message chooses every turn.

Three natural questions: Does it look the same everywhere? Can you get everywhere? How long before two walks meet?

A Cayley graph: vertices are group elements, arrows are multiplication by a fixed generating set. Classic viewpoint (Aldous–Diaconis, Bayer–Diaconis): shuffling is a walk on Sn. The difference here: the walk is not random; the key and the message steer it. The three questions are the outline of the rest: vertex transitivity, connectivity, free depth.

Every deck sees the same web

Proposition: vertex transitivity

For any decks v and w, some symmetry of the graph carries v to w.

Proof: repaint the faces

Cuts and shuffles move positions; they never read what is printed on the cards. Relabel every card by the same rule h, and each arrow d → gkd becomes an arrow dh → gkdh. To send v to w, take h = v−1w. ∎

So

Every question about the graph can be asked from the sorted deck.

Formally: right translation ρh(d) = d∘h is a bijection of Sn, and ρh(gkd) = gkdh = gkρh(d): generators act on the left, h on the right, and multiplication is associative. Taking h = v−1w gives ρh(v) = w. Every Cayley graph has this property. Card picture: the moves only care where cards are, not what they say, so repainting all faces consistently is a symmetry. Subtlety for the sharp student: in the cipher, which arrow you take does depend on the faces (top + x), but the graph’s arrows are labeled by cut position, so the graph itself is face-blind. Consequence: the pictures and counts made from 0123… hold from every deck. Cryptographic reading: the shape of the walk tells you nothing about where it started.

Can the walk reach every deck?

Theorem: every even n

The moves gk generate all of Sn, so the graph is connected on all n! decks.

Shuffle, cut half (h = n/2), unshuffle: the deck reverses.

β−1 R β = (0 1)(2 3)⋯

Conjugate again: swap every pair. Call it x.

With σ = β−1τβ: a single swap.

4

τ−i (0 1) τi = (i i+1)

Cuts slide the swap anywhere. Adjacent swaps sort anything (bubble sort).

τ = t1 is the one-card cut. Setup: β = g0 is a move; β−1 = βord−1 since Sn is finite; τ = β−1g1. So everything is generated by β and τ. Each identity is a direct position-chase valid for every even n (foundations.ltx: Reversal, Pair exchange, A transposition). Alternative finish: the group is primitive and contains a transposition, so it is Sn by Jordan’s theorem. The perfect variant (an extra swap each step) is proven connected for n = 40; all even n is open. Next slide: step 1 with cards.

How long do two walks stay apart?

Free depth r

The largest r with all cut sequences of length ≤ r ending at different decks.

Birthday problem

40k walks land in 40! decks. Expect a collision once (40k)2 ≈ 40!.

Four cards

r = 1: cut 1 twice is already a loop.

½ log40 40! ≈

15

a heuristic, not a theorem

Why care: if two different messages (cut sequences) of length k lead to the same deck, the cipher has merged two paths, which leaks information. Pigeonhole forces a loop by length about 30 (4031 > 40! > 4030). Birthday heuristic: collisions become likely when the number of pairs ~ (40k)2 reaches 40!, so k ≈ ½ log40(40!) ≈ 14.95. For n = 4 the free depth is 1 since g12 = e (the 15 instead of 16 on the web slide).

The shortest loop on 40 cards

(β t2)9 = e

Move two cards to the bottom, shuffle. Do it nine times: you’re back where you started.

Girth exactly 9

No loop of length ≤ 8: meet in the middle, about 2.6 million decks from each side.

Only one

Exactly one loop of length 9, none of length 10. Leak rate ≈ 40−9.

Perfect variant

Add one swap per step: no loops of length ≤ 10 at all.

The colors are the sorted order; watch them scramble and come back. Meet in the middle: a loop of length L ≤ 8 would put some non-identity deck in both the forward ball B4 and the backward ball B4−; both have 1 + 40 + … + 404 ≈ 2.6 million decks, and they meet only at e. So the plain free depth is between 4 and 8. Relator counts c9 = 1, c10 = 0; each relator of length L costs about cL/nL of collisions (leak rate). Perfect variant (swap positions 0 and 19 after the shuffle): c9 = c10 = 0, and its single-cut loops all have length ≥ 30. All machine-checked in the repo’s test suite.

Open questions

?

Every n

The perfect variant reaches all 40! decks. Does it reach all n! for every even n?

?

Prove the 15

Bound the free depth from below by proof, near ½ logn n!, not by computer search.

?

Mixing

Does this steered walk mix as fast as a random shuffle?

All three are graph questions about one explicit Cayley graph of Sn.

1. The plain variant is proven for all even n by closed-form identities; the perfect variant’s entry transposition is n-specific, so a uniform proof needs a new idea. 2. A provable free-depth lower bound would be a girth/expansion estimate for an explicit Cayley graph of Sn at near-optimal scale; related to open problems (Helfgott–Seress–Zuk; Babai–Seress). One idea: Bayer–Diaconis-style bit accumulation, since each inverse back-front step sorts a card into a half. 3. Classical mixing results (Aldous–Diaconis, Bayer–Diaconis, Diaconis–Shahshahani) are for random shuffles; here the walk is deterministic given the input. Good student research projects.
